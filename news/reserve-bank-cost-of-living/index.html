Reserve Bank Advises Australians to Eliminate Non-Essential Spending Such as Pleasure, Rest and Anything That Makes Life Worth Living

3 August 2026 · 4 min

CANBERRA — The Reserve Bank has urged Australians to reduce inflationary pressure by eliminating discretionary expenditure on dining, recreation, hobbies, relationships, sleep and any remaining activity suggesting employment was intended to support a life outside employment.

Speaking after the Bank’s latest monetary-policy meeting, Governor Helena Bracewell said household spending remained “unacceptably persistent”, particularly among Australians who continued to purchase goods and services after paying for housing, utilities, transport and the privilege of reaching their workplace.

“Many households have made commendable progress by cancelling holidays, abandoning home ownership and replacing fresh food with whatever comes in the largest beige packet,” Bracewell said.

“However, we are still seeing concerning levels of discretionary human activity.”

The Bank identified takeaway coffee, family outings, gym memberships, birthday presents, streaming subscriptions and occasional moments of spontaneous happiness as key contributors to excess demand.

Officials were particularly alarmed by evidence that some workers continued to expect a psychological return from their wages.

“A salary is not a voucher redeemable for enjoyment,” Bracewell said.

“It is a mechanism for maintaining sufficient nutrition, shelter and internet connectivity to ensure a person remains available for work on Monday.”

The Bank’s new household guidance, titled Living Within Your Productive Capacity, recommends Australians divide all expenses into two categories: those required to remain employable and those reflecting an irresponsible attachment to being alive.

Under the framework, rent is considered essential because homelessness may affect punctuality.

Food is conditionally essential, provided it is inexpensive, nutritionally adequate and consumed without turning into a social occasion.

Electricity is essential between 6 pm and 11 pm where required for unpaid professional development, answering workplace messages or preparing for the next day.

Heating, cooling and hot water are classified as “comfort-linked consumption behaviours” and should be minimised.

Exercise remains permitted where it improves productivity, but enjoyment derived from exercise must be declared and offset by reducing expenditure elsewhere.

The guidance also recommends that Australians cease referring to weekends as “their time”, describing the phrase as economically misleading.

“Saturday and Sunday are labour-maintenance windows,” the report states.

“They should be used for grocery procurement, laundry, meal preparation, professional certification, recovery from occupational stress and anticipatory anxiety concerning the week ahead.”

Social interaction should be limited to networking, team-building or conversations capable of producing a referral.

The Bank said Australians who felt isolated could attend an employer-sponsored wellness webinar or briefly acknowledge another person while comparing supermarket unit prices.

Bracewell rejected suggestions that households had already cut spending to the bone.

“The bone remains a tradable commodity,” she said.

“We are asking people to consider whether they genuinely require the entire bone.”

Asked how Australians were expected to remain motivated while surrendering every activity that made work tolerable, Bracewell said motivation was not within the Bank’s mandate.

“Our mandate concerns price stability and full employment,” she said.

“It does not concern why anyone would want either.”

Treasurer Martin Kells welcomed the advice, saying households had an important role to play in correcting economic conditions created by institutions, markets and policy settings entirely beyond their control.

“Every Australian can contribute,” Kells said.

“Some will contribute by postponing dental care. Others will contribute by moving back in with their parents at 43. Those fortunate enough to have children can contribute through the intergenerational transmission of financial dread.”

Australian Employers Council chief executive Graham Lort said workers had become confused about the purpose of remuneration.

“Wages were designed to ensure employees return,” Lort said.

“They were never intended to finance competing priorities.”

Sydney renter Carla Pickering said she had already removed most discretionary spending from her life.

“I don’t eat out. I don’t travel. I cancelled the gym. I stopped buying clothes. I see friends at home, but we sit in the dark now because of the electricity bill,” she said.

“I’m not sure what else they want me to cut.”

An RBA spokesperson later clarified that Pickering’s landlord, bank, employer, state and federal government deemed she was already operating at peak economic efficiency.

“In short, she’s right where we want her.”
