Rent-A-Crowd Protests Pauline Hanson

15 August 2026 · 3 min

MELBOURNE — Australia’s permanently available emergency protest population was deployed to the CBD on Thursday after organisers detected a dangerous increase in the number of citizens forming political opinions without first consulting university activist societies.

Hundreds of students abandoned classrooms and converged on the State Library before marching through Melbourne’s CBD as part of a coordinated national protest against Pauline Hanson and One Nation. Similar demonstrations were held in Sydney, Brisbane and other cities.

Witnesses said the mobilisation occurred with the extraordinary speed normally seen when somebody says “Palestine”, “climate emergency” or “Jordan Peterson is speaking nearby”.

“I was at uni eating lunch and suddenly somebody said Pauline Hanson was gaining in the polls,” said protester Mia Fletcher, 19.

“Within eleven minutes I had a professionally printed placard.”

Fletcher said she had attended several previous demonstrations involving causes ranging from Gaza to fossil fuels, refugees, trans rights, university funding and something involving landlords she could no longer precisely remember.

“It’s all connected,” she explained.

“How?”

“Systems.”

The demonstration was organised by groups including the National Union of Students and activist organisations such as Socialist Alternative and High School Anti-Capitalists.

Political sociologist Dr Carla Pickering rejected suggestions that repeatedly seeing the same activist networks organise demonstrations created anything resembling a “rent-a-crowd”.

“That phrase is deeply dismissive,” Pickering said.

“These are completely spontaneous expressions of public outrage coordinated nationally in advance by established political organisations.”

Asked what made them spontaneous, Pickering said the participants genuinely felt the feelings they had been told the rally was about.

Students carried signs denouncing racism, One Nation and Hanson while chanting slogans through the city.

Several demonstrators interviewed by reporters said Hanson posed a threat to migrants, international students and minorities.

Asked to describe one One Nation’s policy that they thought was racist, or xenophobic, a few paused for several seconds before offering generalised concerns about, “history”, and “things they’d seen on Reddit.

One student eventually cited “the thing about immigration numbers”, before admitting they were “not across the specifics”.

Another said Hanson was “basically against multiculturalism”, then clarified that they were “pretty sure that’s still a thing she is against, like generally”.

When pressed further, several demonstrators said they had not read any recent policy statements from One Nation, but were confident the group’s positions remained “racist”.

“I don’t need to read it,” one protester said.

Political observers noted that this confidence in not needing to engage with primary material has become increasingly common in modern protest movements, particularly those organised at short notice between lectures and part-time shifts.

Dr Pickering said this was not unusual.

“Most movements of this kind tend to operate on three informal tiers,” she said.

“At the top are the not-believers — people who are not especially ideologically committed but are willing to fund, endorse, or amplify almost anything that appears socially or politically useful at the time.”

“In the middle are the organisers — the true believers. They are the ones writing statements, coordinating messaging, and maintaining ideological consistency. They are usually the only group who have actually read anything in detail.”

“And at the bottom is the street crew — the people who show up. They are not necessarily aligned on specifics, but they participate in a shared moral atmosphere. They think they are supposed to be there, and they act accordingly in the moment.”

Protest organiser Karen Virtieu-Siegnall said Hanson’s increasing popularity demonstrated the need for stronger civic education.

“We need young people capable of thinking critically.”

Asked whether this included critically examining arguments made by Hanson, she clarified:

“No, obviously not those.”

At publishing time, Australia’s decentralised grassroots protest movement was awaiting the next nationally coordinated instruction telling it where to spontaneously assemble.
