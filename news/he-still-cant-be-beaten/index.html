He Still Can't Be Beaten

23 September 2026 · 3 min

Gary “Angry” Anderson is dead at 79.

For half a century, Anderson fronted Rose Tattoo with the general demeanour of a man who had been informed that the venue had a noise limit and regarded this as a personal challenge.

There were louder singers. There were prettier singers. There were singers whose publicists could probably get them through an entire interview without using a word requiring an apology afterwards.

But there was only one Angry.

He was bald before bald rock singers were bald. Tattooed before tattoos became something your project manager got on a wellness retreat in Noosa. Five-foot-nothing and somehow capable of occupying every cubic metre of a room.

Rose Tattoo emerged from the Australian pub circuit in the 1970s playing hard, ugly, blues-soaked rock and roll at precisely the volume required to permanently rearrange the internal architecture of a suburban hotel.

Nobody listening to Bad Boy for Love, Nice Boys Don’t Play Rock ’n’ Roll, Scarred for Life or We Can’t Be Beaten required further clarification: the band took no prisoners.

They sounded Australian in an era before sounding Australian required a brand strategy. There was no stakeholder consultation about strengthening Australia’s musical storytelling ecosystem.

Some blokes got together, turned the amplifiers up until the glasses moved across the bar by themselves and created something that people still gave a shit about fifty years later.

This was previously known as culture.

Anderson himself proved much harder to compress into the approved celebrity template.

He was a rock singer, actor, television personality, youth advocate and charity worker. He worked with vulnerable young people, publicly discussed his own childhood abuse, raised money for causes ranging from respite accommodation for people living with HIV/AIDS to support for drought-stricken farmers and was appointed a Member of the Order of Australia for his youth advocacy.

He was also opinionated, argumentative, politically inconvenient and occasionally capable of saying things which ensured nobody would ever be able to convert him completely into a commemorative mural approved unanimously by council.

Good.

People are supposed to contain contradictions.

Dead Australians increasingly emerge from the national mourning apparatus having undergone the cultural equivalent of pressure washing. Every rough edge disappears. Every awkward opinion vanishes. Every life becomes a sequence of achievements narrated over piano music.

Angry Anderson should be spared that indignity.

The bloke was called Angry, for crying out loud.

Leave some dirt on him.

Australia will now commemorate Anderson in the traditional national manner: politicians who could not name three Rose Tattoo songs will publish statements describing him as “an icon”; government accounts will call him “a proud Australian voice”; broadcasters will carefully compress fifty unruly years into ninety seconds between the weather and an update about supermarket prices.

Some department will probably describe his career as an important contribution to Australia’s “cultural landscape”.

Rose Tattoo would have called it rock and roll. Then someone would have hit a slide guitar. That seems more appropriate.

Because beneath all the mythology, the tats, the growl and the bloke seemingly constructed entirely from clenched fists, Anderson possessed something increasingly rare in Australian public life: an identifiable personality.

There was no sense that a communications adviser had spent twenty minutes removing all detectable humanity from an opinion before allowing him to say it.

Rose Tattoo reached its fiftieth year in 2026. Anderson was still out there with them, still fronting the band, until a cardiac arrest during their European tour brought the final run to an abrupt halt.

For decades, crowds shouted back a simple declaration at him:

We can’t be beaten.

Biology has now lodged a technical objection. But the song remains correct.

Rest in peace, mate. May you rock, forever and a day.
