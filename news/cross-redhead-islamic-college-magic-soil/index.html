Cross Redhead's Visit To Islamic College Provides More Evidence Australian Soil Is Not, In Fact, Magic

25 July 2026 · 3 min

BRISBANE — Senator Pauline Hanson has provided further evidence that Australian soil does not possess supernatural properties capable of instantly transforming every person who stands upon it into a secular Queenslander named Brett.

Hanson recently visited the Islamic College of Brisbane, an institution that, for some reason, exists, to discuss Islam, multiculturalism and whether Australians should be required to resume pretending a face covering is simply another delightful expression of our shared national character.

Despite arriving with an Israeli-flag pin attached to her clothing, Hanson was not accosted by the occupants, chased from the premises or escorted through a rear exit.

This distinguished the visit from Prime Minister Anthony Albanese’s appearance at Lakemba Mosque in March, where protesters called him a “genocide supporter”, told him to leave and reportedly referred to him as a “putrid dog”, despite Albanese not wearing an Israeli flag, opposing Pauline Hanson and having spent years expressing carefully calibrated concern for every community capable of affecting a marginal seat.

Observers said the contrast demonstrated the rich diversity of Muslim opinion in Australia, ranging from students willing to engage politely with a politician who openly dislikes their religion to adults willing to abuse a prime minister who has spent his career trying not to offend them.

“This proves there is no single Muslim community,” said multicultural engagement consultant Liam Riestead-Schittlieb.

During the exchange, Hanson questioned students and staff about multiculturalism, Islamic clothing and whether loyalties imported from overseas remain imported once the person carrying them receives an Australian tax file number.

School representatives defended their students’ right to maintain their religious and cultural identity while participating fully in Australian society.

Hanson reportedly asked whether “participating fully” still required accepting any identifiable Australian norms, or whether the country had now moved to a more flexible arrangement under which Australia merely supplied roads, welfare, universities, hospitals and diplomatic protection while newcomers supplied the definition of Australia.

A student wearing a niqab challenged Hanson’s views and explained that the covering was her own choice.

Hanson accepted that the student believed this, before asking whether every choice made inside a powerful religious and familial culture became automatically free once described in English.

The room then experienced several seconds of the increasingly rare democratic practice known as “two people disagreeing without demanding an arrest”.

The visit has complicated the longstanding national theory that migration policy requires no serious consideration because everyone eventually becomes Australian through prolonged exposure to Medicare cards, rental inspections and the Bunnings sausage sizzle.

Under the Magic Soil Theory, cultural conflict cannot be imported because Australia occupies a special moral latitude at which tribal loyalties, sectarian hatreds and incompatible social norms dissolve immediately upon baggage collection.

Any evidence to the contrary is generally treated as further proof that Pauline Hanson has caused the phenomenon by noticing it.

“If migrants arrive with attitudes formed over centuries, those attitudes become Australian attitudes the moment they clear immigration,” explained Riestead-Schittlieb.

“That means anyone who objects to them is attacking Australian values.”

Political analysts said her calm reception at the college did not disprove concerns about Islamic integration, just as Albanese being called a putrid dog at Lakemba Mosque did not prove them.

At press time, Albanese was understood to remain committed to community dialogue, provided future dialogue occurred behind a secure perimeter with vetted participants and an emergency rear exit.
